The short answer: use DOCX while a document is alive — being drafted, edited, or collaborated on — and PDF when it's finished and needs to look identical everywhere. That's the whole decision in one sentence. Everything below is just applying it.

## The one-paragraph explainer

A Word document (DOCX) is a set of instructions: "here's the text, here are the styles, flow it to fit." Open it on a different computer with different fonts and it reflows — sometimes subtly, sometimes dramatically. A PDF is a snapshot: "put this exact letter at these exact coordinates." It looks the same on every device ever made. That single difference — reflowable vs. fixed — determines which format every document should live in.

## Use Word (DOCX) when...

- **The document is still being written.** Drafts, manuscripts, reports in progress.
- **Others will edit it.** Tracked changes, comments, and collaborative editing are Word's home turf.
- **It's a template.** Forms, letterheads, and documents others will fill in and adapt.
- **Content matters more than appearance.** The words are the point; the layout can shift.
- **It needs to reflow.** Accessibility (screen readers handle DOCX well), translation workflows, and responsive reading all favor reflowable text.

## Use PDF when...

- **The document is final.** Contracts, invoices, certificates, published reports.
- **It must look identical everywhere.** Resumes, portfolios, brochures, legal filings — if the layout is part of the message, lock it in.
- **You're distributing, not collaborating.** Read-only by design; recipients get exactly what you sent.
- **It needs to print predictably.** What you see is what the printer produces, on any printer.
- **You're archiving.** PDF is the standard for long-term document preservation precisely because it doesn't depend on the software that made it.

## The decision table

| Situation | Format | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Resume / CV | PDF | Formatting locked, looks professional everywhere |
| Contract for signature | PDF | Final, uneditable, prints identically |
| Collaborative draft | DOCX | Track changes, comments, reflow |
| Invoice to a client | PDF | Final record, can't be quietly edited |
| Template for the team | DOCX | Others will adapt and fill it in |
| Published report | PDF | Layout is part of the message |
| Form people fill in | Either | PDF for print forms; DOCX for digital fill-in templates |

## Converting between them

You'll constantly move documents across this boundary. The honest conversion map:

- **Word → PDF:** the safe direction. PDFEdit's [Word to PDF](/tools/word-to-pdf) tool preserves your fonts (matched to metric-compatible equivalents), sizes, colors, bold/italic, alignment, and lists — all in your browser, no Word needed. Skips images and complex elements, flattens tables.
- **PDF → Word:** the lossy direction. [PDF to Word](/tools/pdf-to-word) extracts the text into clean paragraphs with page breaks — the words survive, the layout doesn't. Fine when you need the content; wrong when you need the design.

Convert once, at the boundary between "working" and "finished." Repeated round trips degrade the document each time.

## Common mistakes

**Sending a resume as DOCX.** The recruiter's Word substitutes your fonts, your careful spacing shifts, and on some systems it opens in a compatibility mode that mangles the layout. PDF locks your design in.

**Collaborating in PDF.** "Can you edit this PDF and send it back" is how formatting goes to die. Keep the working document in Word; export the PDF when it's done.

**Archiving important records as DOCX.** In ten years, will that DOCX render the same? PDF was designed for exactly this — it's the archival format.

**Assuming "final" means "PDF" for everything.** A contract template your team reuses monthly should stay DOCX. "Final" means this specific instance is done, not that the document type is finished forever.

## Collaboration workflows: the handoff points

Documents usually cross the Word/PDF boundary at specific moments. Naming them prevents the classic messes:

- **Draft → review:** stays in Word. Reviewers need tracked changes and comments, which PDF can't do well.
- **Review → approval:** still Word, or PDF if approvers should only comment, not edit. Many teams export a PDF for the approval round precisely to prevent silent edits.
- **Approval → distribution:** PDF. This is the point of no return — the document is final, locked, and identical for everyone.
- **Distribution → revision:** back to the Word source. Never "edit the PDF and re-export" as a workflow — always return to the source DOCX, edit there, and export a fresh PDF.

The recurring failure mode is losing the source DOCX and ending up with only the PDF when revisions are needed. **Keep both files**: `report-final.docx` (source) and `report-final.pdf` (distribution). Future revisions will thank you.

## Archiving: why PDF wins long-term

For records you need to open in ten years — tax filings, contracts, certificates — PDF is the safer bet:

- **Self-contained.** Fonts are embedded; the file doesn't depend on Word being installed or on a cloud service existing.
- **Stable rendering.** A PDF from 2006 opens identically today. A DOCX from 2006… usually opens, but formatting drift happens.
- **PDF/A exists.** The archival variant of PDF (ISO standard) is what governments and enterprises use for long-term preservation.

Practical rule: anything you'd put in a fireproof safe, keep as PDF. Anything you're still working on, keep as DOCX. Keep both during the transition.
