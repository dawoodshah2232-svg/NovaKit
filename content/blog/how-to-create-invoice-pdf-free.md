## What the invoice generator actually does

The tool builds the whole document for you from a form:

**Your details and your client's.** Business name, email, phone, and address for you; name, email, and address for the client. Both appear in the PDF's "From" and "Bill To" sections.

**Invoice number, dates, and currency.** A unique invoice number (e.g. INV-2026-001), the invoice date, and the due date. Currency is a proper dropdown: USD, EUR, GBP, CAD, AUD, or JPY, with the correct symbol shown everywhere.

**Tax rate and discount.** Percentage fields. Tax is applied to the subtotal after the discount — the standard order most businesses expect.

**Notes and payment terms.** A free-text area for things like "Payment due within 14 days" or bank transfer details.

**Three design templates.** Clean Minimal (monochrome), Corporate Blue (enterprise look), and Bold Modern (high contrast). The live preview switches instantly so you can compare.

## Create your invoice in 5 steps

**1. Start with your details.** Your name or business name, email, and address. If you're a freelancer without a company address, a city and country line plus email and phone is fine.

**2. Add the client and the numbers.** The invoice number should be unique and sequential — INV-2026-001, INV-2026-002, and so on. Set the invoice date to today and the due date to whatever your terms are (14 or 30 days is common).

**3. Add line items.** One row per deliverable or service. Be specific in descriptions — "Logo design, 3 concepts" beats "Design work" if a client ever questions the bill. Quantity × unit price gives the line total.

**4. Set tax and discount.** Enter your tax rate as a percentage. A discount field is there for early-payment offers or promotions — it's optional and only shown on the invoice if you use it.

**5. Preview, then download.** Flip through the live preview, check the totals, and hit Download. If you spot a mistake later, the form is still there — just fix it and download again. (Note: this is general guidance, not professional tax or accounting advice — if you're unsure about tax obligations in your country, ask a local accountant.)

## Why a PDF, not a spreadsheet or Word file

Three practical reasons:

**It can't reflow.** A spreadsheet invoice can look different on the client's machine — columns shift, fonts substitute, page breaks land in odd places. A PDF looks identical everywhere, on every device, forever.

**It looks intentional.** A generated invoice with a proper layout, aligned totals, and consistent typography signals that you're a serious business. Spreadsheets signal "I'll figure this out later."

**It prints and archives cleanly.** One page, A4, done. Clients can forward it to their finance department, accountants can file it, and you can store it alongside your books without conversion worries.

## Common mistakes to avoid

**Vague line items.** "Services rendered" invites questions and delays payment. Describe what you actually did.

**No due date.** An invoice without a due date is a suggestion. "Net 14" or "Net 30" in the payment terms sets expectations.

**Non-sequential numbering.** Gaps in invoice numbers look sloppy and can raise eyebrows with accountants. Keep them sequential.

**Forgetting to save a copy.** Download the PDF for yourself too, and store it with your records — or protect the final copy with a [password](/tools/protect-pdf) if it contains sensitive details.

## After you send the invoice

Creating the PDF is half the job. The other half is the boring admin that keeps cash flowing:

**Save your copy immediately.** Download the PDF for your own records too, filed by year and number: `invoices-out/2026/INV-2026-001.pdf`. When tax time comes — or when a client claims they never received it — you'll be glad.

**Track what you've sent.** A simple register: invoice number, client, date sent, amount, date paid. Five columns. Update it when you send and when money lands. Overdue invoices become visible at a glance instead of surfacing three months later as a cash-flow surprise.

**Follow up on time.** If payment hasn't arrived a few days after the due date, send a polite reminder with the invoice re-attached. Most late payments are forgetfulness, not malice — a nudge fixes them. If it's still unpaid after another week, escalate politely but firmly.

**Reconcile monthly.** Once a month, check sent vs. paid. This fifteen-minute habit catches errors (wrong amount, wrong client email) while they're still easy to fix.
