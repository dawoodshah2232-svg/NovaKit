The short answer: open PDFEdit's [Image to PDF](/tools/image-to-pdf) tool, add all your images at once, drag the thumbnails into the order you want, and compile — you get one clean multi-page PDF. It runs entirely in your browser, so even a batch of personal photos never uploads anywhere.

## When you'd actually do this

- **Receipts and invoices** for expense reports — one PDF, in date order
- **Photo documentation** — before/after shots, property inspections, insurance claims
- **Portfolios and lookbooks** — design work, photography, real estate listings
- **Scanned pages from a phone camera** — snap each page, combine into one document
- **Screenshots** — a bug report or tutorial assembled page by page

Basically: whenever "a folder of images" needs to become "a document someone can read."

## Step by step

1. **Open the tool.** [Image to PDF](https://www.pdfedit.website/tools/image-to-pdf) — works on phones and desktops.
2. **Add everything at once.** Select all the images in one go from your file picker, or drag them in. JPG, PNG, WebP, BMP, GIF all work in the same batch.
3. **Drag into order.** This is the step people skip and regret. Thumbnails start in the order you added them — rearrange them now, because this becomes your page order.
4. **Compile and download.** One PDF, one page per image, images embedded at full quality.

### Batch tips that save real time

- **Name files in order first.** If your phone saved them as IMG_2041 through IMG_2058, great — select them in order and the tool will likely match. If not, rename quickly before uploading.
- **Mix formats freely.** A batch can include phone JPGs, PNG screenshots, and WebP downloads. The tool normalizes each one appropriately — JPEGs embed untouched, everything else becomes clean PNG data.
- **Watch total size on phones.** The conversion happens in your browser's memory, so a hundred 12-megapixel photos on an old phone will be slow. For giant batches, consider [compressing the PDF](/tools/compress-pdf) afterward or splitting into two documents.
- **Check orientation.** Phone photos sometimes carry rotation metadata. Glance through the thumbnails before compiling.

## What you get (and what you don't)

You get a clean, standard PDF: one image per page, embedded at original quality, no watermark, no account. What you don't get is OCR — the pages are images, so the text in them isn't selectable or searchable. If you photographed document pages and need searchable text, run the result through an [OCR tool](/tools/ocr-pdf) afterward (it works best on clear, high-contrast pages in English, Spanish, French, or German).

And if you ever need the images back out, our [PDF to images](/tools/pdf-to-images) tool renders each page back to PNG or JPG — see the [extraction guide](/blog/pdf-image-extraction-guide) for the honest details on what that recovers.

## Common mistakes

**Emailing 40 separate images.** Nobody wants to open 40 attachments. One PDF, in order, with a sensible filename.

**Forgetting the order.** The number-one redo reason. Five seconds of dragging thumbnails beats recompiling.

**Screenshotting instead of using originals.** If you have the original photos, use them — screenshots of your own photos just add a generation of quality loss.

## Ordering strategies for common cases

The drag-to-reorder step is where batches succeed or fail. Tactics by use case:

- **Receipts for expenses:** chronological, oldest first. Finance teams read top-down; match their expectation.
- **Before/after documentation:** strict pairs — before, after, before, after. Don't group all befores then all afters; the comparison is the point.
- **Photo portfolios:** lead with your strongest image. Nobody promised to look at all forty.
- **Scanned multi-page documents:** page 1, 2, 3… obvious, but double-check — phone cameras don't always save in capture order, and one out-of-sequence page confuses every reader.
- **Mixed sources:** if some images are portrait and others landscape, that's fine — each gets its own page. But eyeball the thumbnails: a landscape photo squeezed onto a portrait page still reads fine, while a sideways image does not.

## After the PDF: useful next steps

The compiled PDF is a starting point, not necessarily the finish:

- **Too big to email?** [Compress it](/tools/compress-pdf) — image-heavy PDFs shrink well.
- **Need to add a cover page or combine with another PDF?** [Merge PDFs](/tools/pdf-merger) afterward.
- **Pages in the wrong order and you already compiled?** Don't reconvert — [reorder the PDF pages](/tools/organize-pdf) directly.
- **Sensitive contents?** [Password-protect](/tools/protect-pdf) the finished document before sharing.

## File naming: the unglamorous superpower

Batches live or die by filenames. Before uploading:

- **Sequential names sort correctly** — `receipt-01.jpg` … `receipt-12.jpg` beats `IMG_2041.jpg` … `IMG_2058.jpg` when you're dragging thumbnails into order.
- **Zero-pad numbers** — `page-09` sorts before `page-10`; `page-9` doesn't (in most file pickers).
- **Dates beat descriptions** — `2026-09-15-invoice.jpg` is findable forever; `scan-final-v2-REALLY-final.jpg` is not.

Two minutes of renaming in your file manager saves ten minutes of squinting at thumbnails. For large archives, consider a small text file in the folder noting what each PDF contains — future searches will thank you for the thirty seconds it takes.
