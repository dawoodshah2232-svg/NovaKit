The short answer: open a JPG to PDF tool in your browser, upload your photos, arrange them in the order you want, and download the PDF. It takes under a minute on a phone or a computer, and you don't need to install anything.

People need this constantly — snapping a photo of a receipt for expenses, capturing a signed contract page by page with a phone camera, turning a stack of kids' drawings into one file for the grandparents. Whatever the reason, the process is the same, and it's simpler than most people expect.

## On your phone

**iPhone:** Open Safari and go to PDFEdit's [JPG to PDF](/jpg-to-pdf) tool. Tap upload, choose your photos from the library (you can select several at once), drag them into order, and hit convert. The PDF downloads straight to your Files app. That's it — no app install, no account.

Your iPhone also has a built-in method: open the Photos app, select the images, tap Share, then Print. On the print preview, pinch outward on the thumbnail with two fingers — the preview turns into a PDF you can save or share. It works, but ordering multiple photos is fiddly, which is why the browser tool is usually faster.

**Android:** Same deal in Chrome — open the converter, upload from your gallery, arrange, download. Most Android phones also have a Print option in the Photos app's share menu that can save as PDF, but the steps vary by manufacturer, and Samsung's menu doesn't look like Pixel's. The browser route is identical on every phone, which is its real advantage.

## On your computer

**Windows:** Open the JPG to PDF tool in Edge or Chrome, drag your images in, arrange them, and download. Windows also has "Microsoft Print to PDF" — select your images in File Explorer, right-click, Print, and choose Microsoft Print to PDF as the printer. Fine for one-offs, clumsy for ordering.

**Mac:** The Preview app does this natively: select your images in Finder, right-click, Open With > Preview, then File > Print > PDF > Save as PDF. It works, but Preview gives you almost no control over page size or image fit. For anything you actually care about, the browser tool's layout options win.

Honestly? The browser method is the same three clicks on every device, which is why we recommend it even on computers that have built-in options.

## Combining several images into one PDF

This is where most people trip up — they convert each photo separately and end up with twelve PDFs instead of one. Upload all the images in a single batch. Each image becomes one page, in the order you arrange them.

Order matters more than you'd think. For a multi-page contract, number your photos as you take them. For expense receipts, put them in date order before you upload — reordering afterward in most tools means starting over. Take thirty seconds to get the sequence right and you'll save yourself the headache.

## Common mistakes

**Using screenshots instead of the original photos.** A screenshot of a photo is a lower-resolution copy of it. If the document matters — an ID, a contract, anything someone else needs to read closely — use the original image file.

**Sideways photos.** Phone cameras usually get orientation right, but scanned or forwarded images often don't. If a page comes out sideways in the PDF, rotate it before you send it — a [PDF rotator](/rotate-pdf) fixes it in seconds. A sideways contract page is a small thing that makes you look careless.

**Enormous file sizes.** Ten 12-megapixel photos make a hefty PDF. If you're emailing it or uploading it to a portal with a size limit, [compress the PDF](/compress-pdf) afterward — you can often cut the size dramatically without any visible difference.

**Forgetting which file is which.** "IMG_4829.pdf" tells future you nothing. Rename the PDF before you send it: "Lease-Agreement-Signed-2026.pdf" takes five seconds and saves everyone confusion.

## What about PNG, HEIC, and other formats?

Most good converters accept PNG too. HEIC — the format iPhones shoot in by default — is the awkward one. The simplest fix: on your iPhone, go to Settings > Camera > Formats and choose Most Compatible, and future photos will save as JPEG. If someone sends you a HEIC file, ask them to resend as JPG, or use a converter that handles HEIC directly.

One more tip: if your "photos" are actually pictures of text documents and you need the text to be searchable or copyable, convert to PDF first and then run [OCR](/ocr-pdf) on the result to extract the text into a separate file. A PDF made from photos is just pictures of pages — OCR reads those pictures and hands you the words as text you can use.

## Before you send it

A quick once-over saves embarrassment. Open the PDF and flip through: are all the pages there, in the right order, right side up? Zoom into one page — is the text sharp enough to read? If a photo looks soft, swap in the original instead of the screenshot.

Then name the file like a human: "Expense-Receipts-March-2026.pdf" beats "combined.pdf" every time. And if the file is still too big for email, compress it — most inboxes choke around 20–25 MB, and a compressed photo PDF usually lands well under that.

Also worth knowing: PDF is the right format when the recipient needs to print or archive. If they just need to glance at the photos on their phone, the original JPGs are fine — PDF earns its keep for printing, signing, and official submissions.

That's really all there is to it. One tool, one batch upload, one PDF — and a file that opens correctly on every device your recipient might use.
