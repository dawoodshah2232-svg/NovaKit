The short version: a QR code turns your business card from a piece of paper into a doorway — scan it and your contact is saved, your portfolio opens, or your booking page loads. Done right, it takes up a corner of the card. Done wrong, it's an unscannable blob.

Business cards haven't died; they've specialized. Nobody keeps a card for the phone number anymore — they keep it for the *person*, and the QR code is what converts that first impression into a saved contact or a visited portfolio before the card gets lost in a bag.

## What should the QR code link to?

**vCard (contact download).** The code contains your name, phone, email, company, and title in the standard vCard format. One scan → "Add to contacts?" prompt. This is the highest-value option for most people, because saving a contact is the one action every recipient might take. Our [QR generator](/tools/qr-generator) has a Business Card preset that builds the vCard format for you — just swap in your own details.

**Your website or portfolio.** Best when your work speaks for itself — designers, photographers, consultants. The recipient sees your best work within seconds of meeting you.

**A booking or calendar page.** For anyone whose business is appointments — coaches, salons, advisors. The card becomes a scheduling tool.

**A link-in-bio style page.** If you live across several platforms (portfolio, LinkedIn, booking, WhatsApp), one QR code to a single landing page beats printing four URLs.

**What not to link:** your generic company homepage (unless that's genuinely the destination), anything that requires a login, or a PDF that downloads instead of opening. Every tap of friction loses people.

## Design rules that keep it scannable

**Size matters.** Minimum 2 × 2 cm on the card. Business cards are small (typically 85 × 55 mm), so the code will occupy a meaningful corner — plan the layout around it rather than squeezing it in.

**Contrast is non-negotiable.** Dark code, light background. Colored codes are fine if the contrast is strong — a navy code on white works; a light-gray code on white does not. Never invert to white-on-dark for print; most scanners struggle with it.

**Give it a quiet zone.** QR codes need a clear margin around them (the generator lets you set the margin size). Don't let text, borders, or design elements crowd the code.

**Keep the background clean.** No photos, gradients, or patterns behind the code. A solid light panel on an otherwise designed card looks intentional and scans perfectly.

**Use vector for print.** Download the code as **SVG**, not PNG — it scales to any size without pixelation, which is exactly what print needs. The [QR generator](/tools/qr-generator) offers both, plus error-correction levels; use Medium or higher so the code survives minor print imperfections.

## Placement and layout

The back of the card is the classic spot — clean, uncluttered, one job. Front-corner placement works if the design is minimal. Either way, add a tiny caption under the code: "Scan to save contact" or "Scan for portfolio." People scan far more when told what they'll get — a bare code is a mystery, a labeled code is an invitation.

If you're doing a vCard code, consider pairing it with your printed details anyway. Some recipients prefer typing; some phones are old. Redundancy on a business card is a feature.

## The vCard details worth getting right

A vCard QR code holds structured fields: name, organization, title, phone, email, URL. A few tips:

- **Use your direct contact**, not a generic info@ address — the point is reaching *you*.
- **One phone number.** Your mobile. Not the office line, not the fax from 2009.
- **Keep it current.** A vCard with your old job title is worse than no code. When your details change, regenerate — it takes a minute.
- **Test before you print 500.** Scan with both an iPhone and an Android. Check that every field lands in the right place in the contacts app.

## Static vs. dynamic QR codes (an honest note)

You'll see "dynamic QR codes" sold as a premium feature. Here's what the terms mean:

**Static QR code** — the destination is encoded directly in the code. What you print is what it does, forever. Free, permanent, private. This is what our [generator](/tools/qr-generator) makes.

**Dynamic QR code** — the code points to the provider's server, which redirects to your destination. You can change the destination later and track scans — but you're dependent on that provider's service, usually via subscription, and every scan phones home to them.

For a business card, static is almost always the right choice. Your contact details don't change weekly, you don't need scan analytics on a card, and a code that works forever with no third party involved is exactly what you want on something you hand to strangers. Save dynamic codes for marketing campaigns where retargeting and analytics justify the trade-offs.

## Measuring whether it works

A QR code on a card is marketing spend (design time, print cost). Sanity-check it:

- **Ask.** When new contacts mention scanning your card, note it. Anecdotal, but real signal.
- **Use a dedicated landing page.** If the code links to a URL, make it a page you can track (e.g. yoursite.com/card) so you see the traffic separately in analytics.
- **A/B your print runs.** Cards with the code vs. without, if you network heavily. The version that generates more follow-ups wins.

Don't overthink it, though. The primary job of the card's QR code is removing friction for the motivated recipient — someone who *wants* to save your contact or see your work. That alone justifies the corner it occupies.

## Print checklist

1. Code generated as SVG, error correction Medium or higher
2. At least 2 × 2 cm, with quiet zone
3. Dark on light, solid background
4. Caption explaining what the scan does
5. Test scan from a real print proof, not just the screen
6. Details current — regenerate if anything changed
