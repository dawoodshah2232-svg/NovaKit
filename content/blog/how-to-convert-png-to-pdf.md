The short answer: open PDFEdit's [Image to PDF](/tools/image-to-pdf) tool, drop in your PNG (or a whole batch), reorder if needed, and download the PDF. PNGs are embedded losslessly — what goes in is pixel-identical to what comes out. No account, no upload, no watermark.

## What formats actually work

The tool accepts more than the name suggests:

- **JPG / JPEG** — embedded directly, zero re-compression
- **PNG** — embedded losslessly, pixel-perfect
- **WebP** — converted to PNG, then embedded
- **BMP, GIF** — also accepted and converted cleanly

So if your screenshots came out as PNG, your camera saved JPGs, and your designer sent WebPs, you can mix them all in one batch without converting anything first.

## Step by step

1. **Open the tool.** [Image to PDF](https://www.pdfedit.website/tools/image-to-pdf) in any browser — phone or desktop.
2. **Add your images.** Drag and drop, or tap to browse. Add one PNG or twenty.
3. **Reorder visually.** Drag thumbnails into the page order you want. Each image becomes one page of the PDF.
4. **Compile and download.** The tool builds a clean PDF and hands it to you. Everything happens on your device.

## One image vs. a batch: what changes

Nothing about the quality — but the workflow differs slightly. A single PNG becomes a one-page PDF, perfect for attaching to forms or emailing. A batch becomes a multi-page document in your chosen order, which is the fastest way to assemble a portfolio, a set of receipts, or a photo document.

The visual reorder step is the one people miss: thumbnails are in the order you added them, which is rarely the order you want. Take five seconds to drag them into place before compiling.

## Common mistakes

**Printing the image instead of converting.** File > Print > Save as PDF works, but it re-renders the image through your printer pipeline — margins get added, scaling gets guessed. A direct converter embeds the image data itself, so the result is cleaner and smaller.

**Converting PNG → JPG → PDF.** Every format hop costs something. Drop the PNG straight into the tool; there's no reason to convert it to JPG first.

**Zipping images and calling it a document.** A ZIP of PNGs isn't a document — recipients can't easily read, sign, or print it as one thing. A PDF is.

**Assuming "image to PDF" means low quality.** It doesn't. PNG embedding is lossless and JPEGs pass through untouched. The PDF is only as good as your source images, but the conversion adds nothing bad.

## What the PDF looks like

Each image gets its own page, sized to fit. The result is a clean, readable PDF — the kind you can attach to a job application, submit to a portal, or archive. ## Preparing your images first

The converter embeds what you give it, so thirty seconds of prep pays off:

- **Check orientation.** A sideways PNG becomes a sideways PDF page. Rotate images in your photo app before converting — fixing it afterward means recompiling.
- **Crop the clutter.** Screenshots with half a notification shade, photos with your thumb in the corner — crop now, not after the PDF exists.
- **Mind the resolution.** A 400×300 thumbnail becomes a blurry full-page PDF. The PDF can only be as sharp as the source image. For document pages, aim for images at least 1500 pixels on the long edge.
- **Watch the color mode.** Grayscale scans and color photos all embed fine, but a dark-mode screenshot (white text on black) prints terribly. Flip to light mode before capturing if the PDF might ever be printed.

## Single image vs. document: naming and sharing

A one-page PDF from a PNG is often destined for someone else — a form upload, an email attachment, a job application. Two things make it land better:

1. **Rename the file.** `IMG_2041.pdf` tells the recipient nothing. `passport-scan.pdf` does. Rename before you send, not after they ask what it is.
2. **Check the size.** A phone photo embedded at full resolution can make a surprisingly large PDF for email. If it's just for screen viewing, it's fine — but if a portal caps uploads at 5 MB and your PDF is 12 MB, run it through a [PDF compressor](/tools/compress-pdf) afterward.

## When PNG is the wrong choice

PNG is lossless, which also means it's big. A phone photo saved as PNG can be 5–10 MB; the same photo as JPG might be 1–2 MB with no visible difference. If your source images are photographs (not screenshots or graphics), consider converting them to JPG *before* making the PDF — or just use the JPGs directly in the tool. The PDF will be dramatically smaller and look identical on screen.

The exception: if the PDF will be printed, keep PNG (or high-quality JPG). Print shows compression artifacts that screens hide.
