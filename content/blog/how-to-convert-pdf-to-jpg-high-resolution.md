## The DPI settings, explained honestly

The tool renders each PDF page to an image at one of three scale settings:

| Setting | DPI | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **1x Standard** | 72 DPI | Quick previews, thumbnails, fast sharing |
| **2x High-Res** (default) | 144 DPI | Web images, slides, social posts, email |
| **3x Ultra** | 216 DPI | Printing, zooming into details, archiving |

A quick note on honesty: some tools promise 300 DPI but quietly upscale a lower render — that's just a bigger blurry file. PDFEdit caps at 216 DPI and renders pages at exactly that resolution, so the pixels are real. For screens, slides, and most everyday printing, 216 DPI is genuinely sharp. If you're doing professional print production that demands true 300 DPI, that's desktop-software territory.

## PNG or JPG?

The same tool offers both formats, and the choice matters:

- **Choose PNG** for text-heavy pages, diagrams, screenshots, and anything with sharp lines. PNG is lossless — edges stay crisp.
- **Choose JPG** for photo-heavy pages or when file size matters. JPGs are much smaller, and the tool's quality slider (default 92%) lets you trade a little sharpness for a much smaller file. At 92%, the difference is nearly invisible.

Rule of thumb: if the page is mostly words and lines, PNG. If it's mostly photos, JPG.

## Common mistakes

**Converting at 72 DPI and wondering why text looks fuzzy.** The default 144 DPI exists for a reason — 72 is a preview setting, not a sharing setting.

**Using JPG for text documents.** Text compresses badly in JPG at lower quality settings — you'll see halos around letters. Switch to PNG for clean text.

**Screenshotting the PDF instead.** A screenshot captures your screen's resolution (and your PDF viewer's rendering quirks). A proper conversion renders at up to 216 DPI — dramatically sharper, and every page, not just the one on screen.

**Forgetting it's a render, not the original image.** This tool draws each page as a new image — it's not extracting the original embedded photos. If you need the original images out of a PDF at their native quality, that's a different job; see our [image extraction guide](/blog/pdf-image-extraction-guide).

## What file sizes to expect

Higher DPI means more pixels, which means bigger files. Roughly speaking, each doubling of DPI quadruples the pixel count. A text page at 144 DPI PNG might be a few hundred kilobytes; the same page at 216 DPI can be over a megabyte. JPGs are much smaller — a photo page at 144 DPI JPG (92% quality) is often under 200 KB.

Practical implications:

- **Emailing images:** stick to 144 DPI JPG unless the recipient needs print quality. Most email clients and chat apps recompress images anyway.
- **Archiving:** 216 DPI PNG. Storage is cheap; re-rendering later is annoying.
- **Web upload:** 144 DPI is the sweet spot — sharp on retina displays without bloating page weight.

If a ZIP of 50 pages comes out huge, that's normal — it's 50 high-resolution images. Convert at 144 DPI instead of 216, or switch PNG to JPG for photo-heavy documents.

### A note on color accuracy

Renders use your browser's canvas pipeline, so colors match what you'd see opening the PDF on the same device — which is what you want for sharing. One subtlety: PDFs with unusual embedded color profiles can render slightly differently across browsers. If color fidelity is critical (brand assets, product photography), compare the render against the PDF opened in a desktop reader before distributing widely.

## Working with long documents

For a 100+ page PDF, a few tactics help:

- **Convert once, at the right setting.** Re-rendering because you picked 72 DPI wastes more time than choosing carefully upfront.
- **Let the ZIP do the work.** Downloading 100 individual images is click-hell; the single ZIP archive is the whole point.
- **Mind browser memory.** Rendering happens on your device. On an older phone, a very long document at 216 DPI may be slow — 144 DPI will fly through the same file.
- **Name the output.** The ZIP arrives with a sensible filename based on your PDF. Rename it before archiving so future-you can find it.
