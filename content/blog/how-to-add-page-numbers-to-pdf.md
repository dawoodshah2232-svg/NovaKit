The short answer: upload your PDF to a page-numbering tool, choose where the numbers appear (bottom center is the classic spot), pick a font and size, and download. Your document comes back identical except for the new numbers in the margins. The whole thing takes about thirty seconds.

Page numbers sound trivial until you don't have them. Try discussing a 60-page contract on a call — "go to the part about termination" — without them. Or printing a report, dropping the stack, and spending twenty minutes putting it back in order. Numbering pages is one of those tiny tasks that prevents real headaches.

## How to add page numbers to a PDF, step by step

1. **Open a page-numbering tool** in your browser. No software to install.
2. **Upload your PDF.** Drag it in or browse your files.
3. **Choose the position.** Bottom center is the standard for reports and books. Bottom-right or top-right works well for business documents. Pick wherever your margins have room.
4. **Set the style.** Choose a font, size, and format — plain numbers (1, 2, 3), or "Page X of Y" so readers know how long the document is.
5. **Set the starting number if needed.** Numbering from 1 is the default, but you can start anywhere.
6. **Apply and download.** The numbers are stamped onto every page and your file is ready.

If your PDF contains anything confidential, use a tool that runs entirely in your browser, like [PDFEdit's page number tool](/add-page-numbers) — your file is processed on your device and never uploaded anywhere.

## Getting the details right

**Position matters more than you think.** The footer center is the safest choice for anything formal. But check your document first: if the footer already has a company name or disclaimer, put the numbers in a corner instead. Overlapping the number with existing text looks sloppy and can make both unreadable.

**Font size: smaller than you think.** Page numbers should be noticeable, not prominent. A size slightly smaller than your body text, in a plain font, is right. Nobody should notice your page numbers until they need them.

**"Page X of Y" vs plain numbers.** For anything people might print or split apart — reports, manuals, proposals — use "Page 4 of 32". It tells the reader immediately if they're missing pages. For short documents under ten pages, plain numbers are fine.

## Numbering part of a document

Sometimes you don't want every page numbered. A thesis might number the main chapters but not the title page. A merged packet might need continuous numbering across files that were numbered separately.

The workaround is simple:

1. [Split](/split-pdf) the PDF into the sections you want numbered and the ones you don't.
2. Add page numbers to just the section that needs them, starting from the right number.
3. [Merge](/merge-pdf) everything back together in order.

It's three quick steps, and it gives you full control over which pages carry numbers.

## Numbering styles for different document types

Not every document wants plain "1, 2, 3" in the footer. Match the style to the context:

**Business reports and proposals:** "Page X of Y" in the bottom center. It looks professional and tells the reader exactly where they are. This is the default you should reach for unless you have a reason not to.

**Legal documents:** some firms ask for Bates numbering — a prefix plus a zero-padded number (like `ABC-0001`). PDFEdit's tool doesn't do custom prefixes, so confirm the format with whoever's managing the case and use dedicated Bates software if a prefix is required.

**Books and theses:** front matter (title page, table of contents) traditionally uses roman numerals (i, ii, iii), switching to arabic numerals (1, 2, 3) for the main body. You can do this by splitting the document, numbering each part separately, and merging.

**Slide decks exported to PDF:** top-right or bottom-right corner, small and unobtrusive. Nobody wants a giant number competing with the slide content.

The throughline: page numbers should be findable in half a second and invisible the rest of the time.

## Fixing or removing page numbers

Added numbers to the wrong position? Wrong starting number? Don't panic — but don't stamp a second set on top either. The clean approach:

1. Go back to your original unnumbered file (you kept one, right?) and re-run the numbering with corrected settings.
2. If you no longer have the original, check whether the numbers were added as a separate layer — some tools let you undo or strip overlay layers without touching the underlying content.

This is why keeping an unnumbered master copy is worth the tiny effort. Name it clearly — `report-final-NONUM.pdf` or similar — before you stamp anything. Future corrections take seconds instead of requiring a rebuild.

## Common mistakes

**Numbering a scanned PDF without checking alignment.** If your scan is slightly crooked, numbers stamped in the margin will look crooked too. Straighten or re-scan first if the pages are visibly tilted.

**Forgetting the cover page.** Many formal documents leave the title page unnumbered, with numbering starting on page 2. Decide this before you number, not after — it's easier to set the range up front than to fix it later.

**Using numbers that clash with existing content.** Some PDFs already have numbers baked in from the original authoring tool. Adding a second set creates a mess. Flip through the document first; if numbers are already there, you may not need to add any at all — or you might need to cover the old ones before stamping new ones.

Page numbering is a two-minute job that makes any long document dramatically easier to use. Do it before you share the file, not after someone asks "which page is that on?"
