The short answer: drop your PDF files into a free online merge tool, drag them into the order you want, click one button, and download the combined document. It takes less than a minute.

Merging PDFs is one of those small tasks that comes up constantly — combining a contract with its appendix, stacking monthly invoices into one file, or joining scanned pages that came out as separate files. Here's how to do it properly.

## The basic process

In [PDFEdit's merge tool](/merge-pdf), it works like this:

1. **Add your files.** Drag two or more PDFs into the browser window, or click to browse. There's no sign-up.
2. **Check the order.** The files merge top-to-bottom in the order shown. Drag them around until the sequence is right — this is the step people skip and regret.
3. **Click Merge PDF.** The tool combines every page into a single document on your device.
4. **Download.** Save the merged file. The originals are untouched.

That's the whole workflow. The merged PDF keeps each file's pages exactly as they were — no reformatting, no quality loss.

## Getting the page order right

Order mistakes are the number one merging headache. A few habits that help:

- **Name files clearly before merging.** "Contract.pdf" and "Contract-Appendix.pdf" are obvious; "scan001.pdf" and "final_final2.pdf" are not.
- **Merge in small batches when it matters.** If you're combining 20 invoices, do them in groups of five and check each batch. Finding one out-of-order invoice in a 60-page file is miserable.
- **Remember it's top-to-bottom.** The first file in the list becomes the first pages of the merged document. Cover page first, appendices last.

If you mess up the order, don't panic — you can [reorganize pages](/organize-pdf) in the merged file afterward, dragging pages into place without starting over.

## Merging scans and mixed files

Real-world merging is rarely two clean PDFs. Common situations:

**Scanned pages as separate files.** Scanners love to output one file per page. If your scans are JPGs, convert each with [JPG to PDF](/jpg-to-pdf) first, then merge the resulting PDFs. If they're already PDFs, merge directly.

**Different page sizes.** Merging an A4 contract with a US Letter appendix works fine — each page keeps its own size. The merged document just has mixed page sizes, which is normal and prints fine.

**Password-protected files.** You'll need to [unlock](/protect-pdf) a password-protected PDF before merging it. The merge tool can't combine a file it can't open.

## What merging doesn't do

A merged PDF is a stack of pages, not a blended document. It won't:

- Renumber pages across the combined file (use [add page numbers](/add-page-numbers) afterward if you need continuous numbering)
- Unify fonts or styles between documents
- Shrink the file — the merged PDF is roughly the sum of its parts. If it's too big to email, [compress it](/compress-pdf) after merging.

## When merging goes wrong

**The merged file is huge.** Ten scanned invoices at 5 MB each become a 50 MB file. Compress after merging — one pass usually cuts it dramatically.

**Pages are upside down or sideways.** Some scans come out rotated. [Rotate the pages](/rotate-pdf) before or after merging; it takes seconds.

**A file won't add.** It's usually either password-protected or corrupted. Try opening it in your regular PDF reader first — if it won't open there either, the file itself is the problem, not the merge tool.

## Merging for specific jobs

Different jobs call for slightly different approaches:

**Job applications.** Recruiters skim. One PDF containing your cover letter, CV, and certificates — in that order — beats four separate attachments every time. Name it something like "Aisha-Khan-Application.pdf" so it's findable in their downloads folder.

**Expense reports.** Receipts arrive as a mess: email attachments, phone photos, scanned slips. Convert the photos to PDF, merge everything in chronological order, and your finance team gets one clean file instead of fourteen. You'll be their favorite employee that month.

**Legal and property bundles.** Contracts with appendices, ID copies, and supporting documents belong in one file. Merge them, then [add page numbers](/add-page-numbers) so everyone can say "see page 12" and mean the same thing.

## Merging on your phone

You don't need a computer for this. Open PDFEdit's merge tool in your phone's browser, tap to add files from your photos or files app, drag them into order, and merge. It works the same as on desktop.

This is genuinely useful when you're away from your desk — say a client calls asking for "everything in one file" while you're in a taxi. Pull the PDFs from your email, merge them on your phone, and send the combined file back before you arrive. Nobody will know you didn't do it at a desk.

One tip for mobile: merging works best on Wi-Fi or a decent connection for very large files, since the browser is doing the heavy lifting on your device. For typical documents under 20 MB, you won't notice any difference.

## What if you need to split it again later?

It happens — you merge twelve invoices, then the client asks for March separately. No need to start over. Open the merged file in a [split tool](/split-pdf), pull out the pages you need, and save them as a new file. Merging isn't permanent, so don't stress about getting it perfect on the first try.

## The bottom line

Merging PDFs should be boring: add files, order them, click merge, download. The only real skill is checking the order before you click. Everything else — mixed sizes, scans, follow-up compression — is a solved problem with the right free tools. Keep your originals, verify the result, and you're done.
