The short version: need to hit a word limit? Use a word counter. Need to understand or improve your text? Use an analyzer. Most writing tasks benefit from the analyzer; the counter is for the moments when the number is the entire assignment.

They look similar — both take text, both return numbers — but they answer different questions. Knowing which question you're asking saves you from using the wrong tool.

## What a word counter does

Counts words. Sometimes characters, sometimes characters without spaces. That's the job, and for its job it's perfect:

- **Essays and assignments** with word limits ("1,500 words ±10%")
- **Abstracts and submissions** with hard caps
- **Client briefs** specifying length ("800-word article")
- **Social posts** with character limits
- **Translation quotes**, which are often priced per word

When the deliverable is defined by length, the count is all you need. Anything more is noise.

## What a text analyzer adds

Our [text analyzer](/tools/text-analyzer) starts with the count and keeps going:

| Metric | Word counter | Text analyzer |
|---|---|---|
| Words | ✓ | ✓ |
| Characters (with/without spaces) | sometimes | ✓ |
| Sentences & paragraphs | ✗ | ✓ |
| Reading time (~225 wpm) | ✗ | ✓ |
| Speaking time (~140 wpm) | ✗ | ✓ |
| Keyword density (top 10) | ✗ | ✓ |
| Avg words per sentence | ✗ | ✓ |
| Avg characters per word | ✗ | ✓ |

Each extra metric answers a real editing question:

- **Sentences + avg words/sentence** → "Is this readable?" (see our [readability guide](/how-to-analyze-text-readability))
- **Reading time** → "Will anyone finish this?" A 12-minute article for a 3-minute audience is a mismatch you can spot before publishing.
- **Speaking time** → "Does my script fit the slot?" At ~140 wpm, a 5-minute talk is ~700 words. Write 1,200 and you'll be speed-talking.
- **Keyword density** → "Am I overusing words?" Every writer has crutch words; the density table finds yours. For SEO, it shows whether target terms appear at natural levels (1–3%) rather than stuffed.
- **Characters without spaces** → the number some platforms and translators actually bill on.

## When to use which

**Use a word counter when:** the requirement is a number. School, submissions, briefs, billing. Check the count, move on.

**Use a text analyzer when:** you're writing to be *read*. Blog posts, marketing copy, reports, scripts, newsletters — anything where readability, length-fit, and word choice affect the outcome. Also for editing other people's text: the metrics give you objective feedback ("average sentence is 27 words") instead of vague notes ("feels dense").

**Use both when:** you're a professional writer. Count for the brief, analyze for the craft.

## The privacy angle

Here's something people don't think about: pasting a draft into a random online tool sends your unpublished work to someone's server. Client copy, unreleased announcements, personal writing — all transmitted. Our analyzer runs 100% in your browser's local memory: the text never leaves your device. For sensitive drafts, that difference matters more than any feature list. If you use other tools, check their privacy claims before pasting anything you wouldn't publish.

## A writer's workflow: when to use each

Here's how the two tools fit into a real writing process:

**Drafting.** Write freely. Don't count anything — counting while drafting is how you get stilted prose.

**First check (analyzer).** Paste the draft in. Note the reading time (does it fit the brief?), average sentence length (readable?), and keyword density (any crutch words or stuffing?). These three numbers tell you what kind of edit you're in for.

**Editing.** Fix what the numbers flagged. Split long sentences, vary overused words, cut or expand to hit the right reading time.

**Second check (analyzer).** Re-paste. Confirm the averages moved in the right direction. Two passes is usually enough — diminishing returns after that.

**Final check (counter logic).** Now verify the hard requirement: word count within the brief's range, character count under the platform limit. The number is the deliverable spec; everything before it was craft.

**Publishing.** For web publishing, one last glance at paragraph count and structure — is it skimmable? Then ship it.

The counter answers "does it fit?" The analyzer answers "does it work?" Ask them in that order and you'll stop doing five anxious re-reads hoping to catch problems the numbers would have shown you instantly.

One last practical note: both tools are only as honest as your input. Analyze the final draft, not an early version — and if you edit after analyzing, re-run the check. A single added paragraph can shift averages enough to matter, especially in short pieces where every sentence carries weight.

It's also worth analyzing competitors' content occasionally — not to copy it, but to calibrate. If the top-ranking articles for your topic average a 3-minute reading time and yours is 12, you've learned something about what the audience expects before you publish a word. Benchmarks beat gut feelings every time.

## The honest bottom line

A word counter is a ruler. A text analyzer is a diagnostic. You don't need the diagnostic to measure a bookshelf — but you do need it when the writing isn't working and you can't tell why. For everyday writing that needs to land with readers, [run it through the analyzer](/tools/text-analyzer): the numbers will tell you what's wrong faster than re-reading it five times.
