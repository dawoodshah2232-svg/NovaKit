A CV is the highest-stakes document most people ever make, and it's usually built in the worst possible way: a Word template from 2014, wrestled into shape, exported with fingers crossed. There's a better path, and it takes about twenty minutes.

## Why PDF is the format (and what "real PDF" means)

Send your CV as a PDF because it looks identical everywhere — the recruiter's phone, the hiring manager's laptop, the ATS parser. A Word CV can reflow into chaos on any of them.

But not all PDF exports are equal. Some "free CV makers" export what is essentially a screenshot — a flat image of your CV. That breaks ATS parsing (there's no text to read), breaks text selection, and looks blurry when zoomed. Our builder exports a **real PDF**: selectable text, embedded fonts, correct A4 pages. Recruiters can copy your email address straight from the document, and ATS systems can actually parse it.

## The 8 templates, honestly described

The builder includes 8 original templates across styles: corporate, minimal, creative, executive, student, technical, marketing, and ATS-friendly. Which to pick:

- **Applying through a portal or large company?** Use the ATS-friendly option. Single column, standard fonts, no graphics, real text — exactly what parsers handle best. Fancy layouts with tables, text boxes, and icons are where ATS parsing goes to die.
- **Sending directly to a hiring manager or small company?** A human reads it first, so a designed template (corporate, minimal, creative) is fine — and a distinctive, professional layout helps you stand out.
- **Student or early career?** The student template is structured for limited experience — education first, projects and skills given proper weight.
- **Technical roles?** The technical template gives skills and projects the prominence they deserve.

## Writing the content (the part that actually gets you hired)

The template is packaging; the words do the work.

**Contact header.** Name, phone, email, city, LinkedIn URL. That's it. No full street address (privacy), no date of birth, no marital status.

**Professional summary (3–4 lines).** Who you are, years of experience, 2–3 key strengths, what you're looking for. Tailor it per application — this is the highest-leverage paragraph on the page. The builder offers honest writing helpers: summary phrasing ideas and skill suggestions based on your role, clearly labeled as suggestions — starting points, not fabrications.

**Experience (reverse chronological).** For each role: title, company, dates, then 3–5 bullet points. Each bullet should follow the pattern *action + result*: "Redesigned onboarding flow, cutting signup drop-off by 30%." Numbers beat adjectives every time. "Responsible for managing" is dead language; "Led a team of 5" is alive.

**Skills.** A clean list or grouped by category. Match the job posting's keywords — this is both human-readable and ATS-relevant.

**Education.** Degree, institution, year. Recent graduates put this near the top; experienced hires move it down.

**Photo (optional, regional).** You can upload a photo — it's auto-resized for a crisp file — but know your market: photos are expected in much of Europe, the Middle East, and Asia, and discouraged in the US, UK, and Canada (to avoid bias in hiring).

## Beating the ATS (without gaming it)

Applicant tracking systems reject qualified candidates every day — not from malice, but from parsing failures. Here's how to be machine-readable without writing for robots:

**Use standard section headings.** "Experience," "Education," "Skills." Creative headings ("My Journey," "Superpowers") confuse parsers looking for expected labels. Save creativity for the bullet content.

**Mirror the posting's keywords — honestly.** If the job asks for "stakeholder management" and you've done it, use that phrase. ATS systems match keywords; humans appreciate the alignment too. Never list skills you don't have — it fails at the interview.

**No tables, text boxes, or graphics for portal applications.** This bears repeating because it's the #1 parsing killer. Single column, linear flow, real text. The ATS Plain template exists for exactly this.

**Standard fonts, standard characters.** Fancy bullets (➤ ✦ ❖) can parse as garbage. Simple round bullets or hyphens. Avoid special characters in general.

**Dates in a consistent, parseable format.** "Jan 2022 – Mar 2024" everywhere. Parsers extract employment timelines from dates; inconsistent formats break the timeline.

**The PDF itself matters.** An ATS needs to extract text from your file. A real PDF with selectable text (what our builder exports) parses cleanly. A scanned image or flattened graphic of your CV parses as nothing. Always verify your exported PDF has selectable text — try copying a sentence from it.

One nuance: some very old ATS setups prefer .docx. If the posting explicitly requests Word, send Word. Otherwise, PDF is the safer default — and our [PDF vs Word guide](/blog/pdf-vs-word-format) explains why.

## Before you send it

- **File name:** `Firstname-Lastname-CV.pdf`. Not `CV_final_v3_REALLYFINAL.pdf`. Recruiters download dozens; make yours findable.
- **Check the PDF, not the editor.** Open the downloaded file and confirm everything rendered — photo, page breaks, special characters.
- **Tailor per application.** One generic CV sent 50 times loses to 10 tailored CVs. Mirror the job posting's language in your summary and skills.
- **Pair it with a clean email or cover note.** The CV gets you considered; the note gets it opened.
