You've polished every bullet point, tuned your summary, and exported your CV as a PDF — the professional choice. You hit submit. And then... silence.

Here's the uncomfortable part: in many hiring processes, your CV never reaches a human at all. Applicant tracking systems (ATS) — the software companies use to collect and filter applications — read your CV first. They scan it as text, pull out your name, contact details, job titles, and skills, and decide whether you match the role. If the software can't read your file properly, your application can be filtered out without anyone ever knowing. No feedback, no second chance.

The good news? Most of the problems that trip up an ATS come from the PDF itself, not from your experience. And they're fixable. Here are the seven most common CV PDF mistakes that cause silent rejections — and what to do about each one.

## 1. Your CV Is Actually an Image, Not Text

This is the single biggest killer. If you scanned a printed CV, exported it from a design tool as a flat image, or saved a screenshot as a PDF, there is no text in your file at all — just pixels. An ATS looking for the word "marketing" will find nothing, because to the software your CV is a picture of words, not words.

**How to check:** Open your PDF, try to select a sentence with your mouse. If you can't highlight individual words, it's an image.

**The fix:** Re-export from the original document (Word, Google Docs, or a proper CV builder) as a real text PDF. If all you have is the scan, run it through OCR (optical character recognition) first to convert the image into selectable text — then proofread it carefully, because OCR makes mistakes with names and formatting.

## 2. Tables and Text Boxes Scramble the Reading Order

Fancy templates love tables: one column for dates, one for job descriptions, a neat little box for your contact details. They look great on screen. But many ATS read a PDF in a simple top-to-bottom, left-to-right order — and tables can get read cell by cell in an order that makes no sense. Your "Senior Designer, 2021–2024" might get parsed as "2021–2024 Senior" on one line and "Designer" three paragraphs later.

Text boxes are worse. Content inside a floating text box is often read completely out of sequence, or skipped entirely.

**The fix:** Build your layout with simple paragraphs, bold headings, and bullet points — not tables or floating boxes. Need structure? Use tabs and spacing, or a simple list format. The copy-paste test (see the FAQ above) will show you exactly what the ATS sees.

## 3. Contact Info Hidden in Headers and Footers

Putting your name, phone number, and email in the page header feels tidy. The problem: many ATS parsers ignore headers and footers altogether. That means the software may file your CV without any contact details — which is an easy reason to be skipped, since the system literally can't reach you.

**The fix:** Put your contact information in the main body of the document, right at the top — name, phone, email, location, and LinkedIn or portfolio link as plain text. You can still style it; just don't trap it inside a header or footer element.

## 4. Fancy Fonts That Don't Embed Properly

That stylish display font might look distinctive on your machine. But PDF fonts need to be embedded in the file to display (and parse) correctly everywhere else. If a font isn't embedded and the ATS doesn't have it installed, your text can render as blank boxes or get misread — turning "Project Manager" into gibberish.

**The fix:** Stick to standard, widely supported fonts: Arial, Calibri, Georgia, Helvetica, Times New Roman, or similar. If you export from Word or Google Docs, the default export embeds these reliably. Avoid decorative or script fonts anywhere in the document — they're a readability risk for humans too.

## 5. Skills Shown as Graphics Instead of Text

Skill bars, star ratings, donut charts, word clouds — they're popular in modern CV templates, and they mean absolutely nothing to an ATS. The software can't interpret a graphic showing "Python: 4 out of 5 stars." It just sees an image and moves on, which means your actual skills never get recorded.

**The fix:** Write your skills as plain text. A simple "Skills" section with a comma-separated or bulleted list — "Python, SQL, data visualization, stakeholder management" — is parsed perfectly. Use the exact terms from the job description where they're genuinely true of you; that's what matching algorithms look for.

## 6. Multi-Column Layouts That Merge Into Nonsense

Two-column CVs look modern and fit a lot on one page. But many parsers read straight across the page, line by line. So "Project Lead" from the left column and "Fluent in Spanish" from the right column get mashed into a single line: "Project Lead Fluent in Spanish." Multiply that across a whole page and your experience section becomes unreadable soup.

**The fix:** Use a single-column layout for the main content. If you really want a sidebar design, keep it minimal — and always run the copy-paste test to confirm the text still flows in the right order. When in doubt, single column wins. Clarity beats cleverness when software is doing the first read.

## 7. Wrong File Type or a Corrupted Export

Some application portals are picky: they ask for PDF and reject anything else, or they have file-size limits that silently fail on large files. A corrupted export — from an interrupted save, an odd converter, or an oversized file full of high-resolution images — may upload fine but fail to parse, leaving you with a blank record in the system.

**The fix:** Export as PDF (not .doc, .pages, or an image format) unless the job posting says otherwise. Keep the file under a few megabytes — compress images before embedding them. After exporting, always reopen the file yourself and check that every page renders correctly. If a portal gives you a preview after upload, actually look at it.

## The 2-Minute Pre-Submit Checklist

Before you send your CV anywhere, run through this:

- [ ] I can select and highlight individual words in the PDF (it's real text, not an image)
- [ ] Copy-pasting the whole CV into a plain text editor produces clean, correctly ordered text
- [ ] Contact details are in the main body, not in a header or footer
- [ ] No tables, text boxes, or multi-column sections control the layout
- [ ] Skills are written as text, not shown as bars, stars, or charts
- [ ] Fonts are standard and the file opens correctly on another device
- [ ] File is a PDF, under a few MB, and every page renders properly

If your CV passes all seven checks, both the ATS and the human recruiter who reads it next will get the best possible version of you.
