The short answer: open the PDF in [PDFEdit Studio](/studio), pick the **Hi-Lite** tool, and drag across any text. Choose your color, export, and the highlights are baked into the PDF permanently.

## Highlight in three steps

1. Open [PDFEdit Studio](/studio) and load your PDF.
2. Select **Hi-Lite** from the toolbar (it's the highlighter icon).
3. Drag over the passage. Adjust the color from the options panel — yellow is the default.
4. Export. Done.

Your highlights land exactly where you dragged, and they're written into the exported PDF as part of the page — visible in Adobe Acrobat, Preview, Chrome, or any other reader. Nothing is final until export: select any highlight to move or delete it, or undo step by step.

## Why your highlighter sometimes "doesn't work"

The most common cause: **the PDF is a scanned image**. A scan is a photograph of a page — the words are pixels, not text, so text-aware tools have nothing to grab. Two fixes:

- **Run OCR first.** PDFEdit's [OCR tool](/tools/ocr-pdf) adds a real, selectable text layer under the scan. Then highlighting works normally.
- **Highlight visually anyway.** Studio's Hi-Lite places a translucent band where you drag, which works on scans too — it just won't snap to word boundaries the way it does on real text.

The other common cause is a locked or restricted PDF. If the file has editing restrictions, [unlock it](/tools/unlock-pdf) first (you'll need the password — it's your file, after all).

## Highlight colors that mean something

Random rainbow highlighting is decoration. A tiny color system turns highlights into a language:

- **Yellow** — key information, quotable passages, "remember this."
- **Pink/red** — problems, disputes, "fix this."
- **Green** — approved, agreed, "this is settled."
- **Blue** — follow-ups, references, "check this later."

Tell your collaborators the key once — in the email, on the cover page — and stick to it. Three or four colors maximum; more than that and nobody remembers what anything means.

## Highlighting for study and research

Students and researchers highlight differently from reviewers: you're building a map you'll navigate later. A few habits that pay off:

- **Highlight less than you think.** If everything is yellow, nothing is. Aim for one or two passages per page — the sentence you'd quote, the number you'd cite.
- **Pair highlights with margin notes.** A highlight says "important." A short text-box note next to it says "important *because*..." — and future-you will be grateful. See our [annotation guide](/blog/how-to-annotate-a-pdf) for the full markup workflow.
- **Export before you close the tab.** Highlights exist as editable layers until export. Make downloading the highlighted file part of the routine, not an afterthought.

## When highlighting isn't enough

Highlighting draws attention; it doesn't remove, hide, or certify anything. If you need to permanently black out sensitive text, that's [redaction](/tools/redact-pdf) — a different, stricter process. If you need to prove a document hasn't changed, that's [password protection](/tools/protect-pdf). Highlighting is the lightest touch in the toolbox: use it for reading and reviewing, not for security.

## Precision tips: highlight exactly what you mean

Sloppy highlighting undermines the point — a highlight that starts mid-word and swallows half the next paragraph says "I was in a hurry," not "this matters."

- **Zoom in for tight passages.** At 150% zoom, starting and ending the drag exactly on word boundaries is easy. At 75%, it's luck.
- **Highlight the phrase, not the sentence.** "Revenue grew 34% in Q3" is a highlight. The entire paragraph explaining revenue is a reading assignment.
- **One continuous drag per passage.** Lifting and re-dragging creates overlapping bands with darker stripes where they overlap. A single smooth motion gives a clean, even mark.
- **Fix mistakes immediately.** Mis-highlighted something? Select it and delete (or undo) right away. A document with six stray highlight fragments looks neglected.

## When to use something other than highlight

Highlighting is the default, but two siblings deserve mention:

**Underline** (via the draw tool or a shape line) works better on documents that will be photocopied in black and white — yellow highlight vanishes on a mono copier, while underlines survive.

**Text-box notes** beat highlights when the point needs explanation. A highlight says "look here." A highlight plus "this contradicts page 4" says "look here, *because*." For review work, the combination is the professional standard — see our [annotation guide](/blog/how-to-annotate-a-pdf).

And when the goal is removing text from the document rather than drawing attention to it, that's [redaction](/tools/redact-pdf) — the opposite operation, with much stricter rules.

## Highlighting in shared reviews

When multiple people highlight the same document, a little coordination prevents chaos:

- **Agree on the color key up front.** Yellow = important, red = issue, green = approved — decided before anyone opens the file, not discovered afterward.
- **Highlight, don't decorate.** In shared reviews, restraint is a courtesy. Ten precise highlights get read; a hundred get ignored.
- **Pair highlights with your initials on contested points.** A red highlight plus "DS: verify against source" tells the next reviewer who raised it and what to do — no meeting required.
- **Consolidate before the next round.** One person merges everyone's highlights into a single master with the notes resolved or carried forward. Three conflicting highlighted copies circulating by email is how things get missed.

The underlying principle: highlighting is cheap to add and expensive to interpret. Every highlight should earn its place by making the next reader's job easier, not busier.
