The short answer: annotation means marking up the page itself — highlights, margin notes, arrows, and stamps. Open the PDF in [PDFEdit Studio](/studio), use the highlight, text, draw, shape, and stamp tools, then export. Your markup becomes part of the PDF.

## What "annotate" really means on a PDF

People say "annotate" but mean different things: highlight key passages, scribble questions in the margins, circle problems, stamp a status, collect feedback. The common thread is **visible markup on the page** that communicates with the next reader.

One honest note up front: PDFEdit Studio doesn't have threaded comment bubbles or sticky notes. Instead, you annotate directly on the page — a yellow highlight on the clause, a text note in the margin saying "confirm this figure," a red arrow pointing at the problem. For review workflows, on-page markup is usually clearer anyway: the comment sits exactly where it belongs.

## The five markup tools and when to use each

**Highlight (Hi-Lite).** Drag over text to mark it. Use it for the passages that matter — the number in the report, the clause under dispute. Studio caps highlight opacity so the text underneath stays readable.

**Text boxes.** Your margin notes. "Needs a source," "client approved 2026-09-20," "check against Q3 figures." Keep notes short; a text box isn't an essay.

**Draw.** Freehand ink for circling, underlining, and quick arrows. Best on touchscreens with a stylus, workable with a mouse.

**Shapes.** Rectangles, lines, and arrows when you want markup that looks deliberate rather than sketched. Arrows are the underrated hero of document review — they connect a margin note to the exact word it refers to.

**Stamps.** "DRAFT," "APPROVED," "CONFIDENTIAL," "REVIEWED" — one click, instant status. Stamps live in the toolbar and place like any other layer.

## A review workflow that actually works

**1. First pass: highlight only.** Read through and highlight everything that needs a decision or a second look. Don't write anything yet — you're mapping the territory.

**2. Second pass: notes and arrows.** Go back through the highlights and add text-box notes. What needs to change? What's the question? Draw an arrow from the note to the exact clause.

**3. Stamp the status.** Put the document's state on the cover page — DRAFT for review, a date stamp, or your initials. Whoever opens it knows instantly where it stands.

**4. Export and send.** Every layer exports into the PDF permanently. The recipient opens it in any reader — Adobe, Preview, Chrome — and sees exactly your markup. Undo and the Inspector are there while you work, so nothing is final until export.

## Tips for markup people can actually read

- **Color-code with discipline.** Yellow for "look at this," red for "problem," green for "approved." Say the key once in your cover note, then be consistent.
- **Keep highlights short.** Highlighting whole paragraphs trains readers to ignore your highlights. One sentence, one number, one clause.
- **Put notes in the margin, not over the text.** Text boxes that cover the original words force readers to move things around. The margin is your canvas.
- **Date your review.** A "Reviewed 2026-09-23 — DS" stamp turns markup from graffiti into a record.

## Annotating on mobile vs. desktop

The workflow above assumes a desktop, but plenty of reviewing happens on phones. The honest comparison:

**Desktop** wins for long documents: precise text-box placement, easier arrow drawing with a mouse, and the Inspector panel for managing dozens of layers. If you're reviewing a 40-page contract, do it here.

**Mobile** wins for speed: open the PDF in your phone's browser, highlight with a finger drag, drop quick text notes. Studio's toolbar collapses into a touch-friendly bottom bar with the same tools. It's ideal for "read on the train, mark the three things that matter" reviews.

One practical tip for mobile: **rotate to landscape** when placing text boxes. The extra horizontal room makes a surprising difference to placement accuracy.

## Sharing annotated PDFs: basic etiquette

Markup is communication, and a few conventions keep it professional:

- **Identify yourself and the date.** A small "Reviewed by DS — 2026-09-23" note on the first page turns anonymous markup into an accountable record.
- **One reviewer, one color family** — or at least one round of review per exported file. When three people's red highlights interleave, nobody knows who said what. Have each reviewer mark up their own copy, then consolidate.
- **Export before sharing, obviously** — but also **name the file well**: `contract-DS-review-2026-09-23.pdf` tells the recipient everything. `contract-final-v7.pdf` tells them nothing.
- **Keep your layered master.** Export a flattened copy for sharing, but keep the Studio session's editable version until the review round closes. Requested changes to wording are trivial before export and annoying after.

## Export settings that matter

When the review is done, the export is the deliverable — get it right:

- **Export at full quality.** Markup is vector layers; there's no reason to downsample. If the tool offers quality options, keep them high — your highlights and notes should print crisply.
- **Flatten review copies.** Once a review round closes, [flatten](/tools/flatten-pdf) the shared copy so nobody can silently delete your comments. Keep the layered master until the round is truly over.
- **Check the file on another device.** Open the exported PDF on your phone or in a different reader before sending. Layer rendering is standardized, but a 10-second sanity check catches the rare surprise.
- **Mind the file size for email.** Heavily annotated scans can get large. If it won't attach, [compress](/tools/compress-pdf) — annotations survive compression fine.
