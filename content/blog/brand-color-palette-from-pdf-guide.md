The short version: extract the colors from the PDF, sort them into roles (primary, secondary, accent, neutrals), check contrast for readability, and write down the final codes. A brand palette is a decision, not just a collection of pretty colors.

Every brand has a PDF somewhere — a brochure, a pitch deck, an old letterhead — that *is* the brand's visual identity, even if nobody ever wrote the colors down. Rebuilding the palette from that document is one of the most useful small projects a business can do, and it takes an afternoon, not an agency retainer.

## Step 1: Extract the raw colors

Start with the extraction workflow from our [color extraction guide](/blog/how-to-extract-colors-from-pdf): export the key PDF pages as images (logo page, cover, a typical content page) and run them through the [Color Palette Extractor](/tools/color-extractor). You'll get dominant colors plus the most prominent colors per page, each with HEX and RGB codes.

Extract from **multiple pages**, not just one. A cover might show the primary and accent; an interior page might reveal the neutrals used for body text and backgrounds. Collect everything first, decide later.

## Step 2: Assign roles

A palette isn't a grab bag — each color has a job. Sort your extracted colors into:

**Primary (1–2 colors).** The brand's signature — the logo color, the main headings, the color people associate with the business. If the PDF's covers and headers keep returning to one color, that's your primary.

**Secondary (1–2 colors).** Supporting colors for subheadings, charts, section backgrounds. They should harmonize with the primary without competing with it.

**Accent (1 color).** The sparing one — calls to action, highlights, links, small details. Accents work best with high contrast against the primary (a warm accent on a cool primary, for example).

**Neutrals (2–3 colors).** Dark for body text, mid-gray for secondary text and borders, light/off-white for backgrounds. Most PDFs already contain these — body text color, page background, table lines. Don't skip neutrals; they're the colors you'll use most.

**What to cut:** near-duplicates. Extraction often surfaces five slightly different blues from scans, gradients, and anti-aliasing. Pick the truest one and drop the rest. A palette with 12 colors is a palette nobody follows.

## Step 3: Check contrast and accessibility

This is where amateur palettes fail. A beautiful brand color that can't hold readable text is a liability.

- **Body text** needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background (WCAG AA standard).
- **Large headings** need at least 3:1.
- **Test your actual combinations** — primary-on-white, white-on-primary, accent-on-neutral — with any online contrast checker. It takes minutes.

Our extractor gives you a text-color suggestion per color (light or dark text), which is a useful first signal — but run the real ratios before finalizing.

Also consider color blindness: roughly 1 in 12 men has some form of it. If your primary and accent are red and green, a chunk of your audience can't tell them apart. Never encode meaning in color alone — pair it with labels, icons, or patterns.

## Step 4: Document the palette

A palette that lives in someone's head doesn't exist. Write it down:

- **Name each color** — "Brand Navy," "Signal Coral," "Paper White." Names make colors discussable.
- **Record HEX and RGB** for digital use.
- **Add CMYK or Pantone equivalents** for print. Screen colors and ink colors are different physics; a serious brand specifies both. (Your print shop can convert, but specifying it yourself avoids surprises.)
- **Note the usage rules** — one line each: "Brand Navy: headings, logo, primary buttons. Never body text." "Signal Coral: accents and CTAs only, max 10% of any layout."
- **Save it as a PDF** — a one-page brand sheet. Ironic, fitting, and practical: it's the document you'll hand to every designer, printer, and freelancer from now on. You can even [protect it](/tools/protect-pdf) if it contains proprietary specs.

## Common palette mistakes

**Too many colors.** The extracted set is raw material, not the final answer. Edit ruthlessly.

**No neutrals.** A palette of four saturated brand colors and no grays produces documents that scream. Neutrals do the quiet work.

**Trendy over timeless.** That neon gradient looks current today and dated in eighteen months. Primaries should be boring enough to last a decade; let accents carry the trend.

**Inconsistent application.** The palette only works if everyone uses it. The brand sheet exists to be referenced — link it in onboarding docs, pin it in the design channel, attach it to freelancer briefs.

## Testing your palette in the real world

A palette that looks good as swatches can fail in practice. Before finalizing, stress-test it:

**The grayscale test.** Convert your palette to grayscale. If the colors become indistinguishable, you're relying on hue alone to separate elements — a problem for color-blind users and monochrome printing. Vary lightness as well as hue.

**The tiny-text test.** Set your body text color on your background color at 12px and read a paragraph. Then try your secondary text color. If either strains, adjust — these are the colors you'll use most.

**The chart test.** Mock up a bar or line chart using only palette colors. Can you distinguish 4–5 series at a glance? If not, your secondaries are too close together.

**The dark-mode test.** If your brand appears in dark interfaces (apps, slides, video), check the palette on dark backgrounds too. Some primaries that sing on white disappear on black.

**The photocopy test.** Print the palette in black and white. Old-school, but it reveals whether your materials survive the worst-case reproduction — fax machines may be dead, but cheap printers aren't.

**Fresh eyes.** Show the palette applied to a mockup (not just swatches) to someone unfamiliar with the project. Ask what it feels like, not whether they "like" it. "Trustworthy," "energetic," "cheap" — the adjectives tell you if the palette matches the brand's intended personality.

Palettes are cheap to revise now and expensive to revise after launch — after the website, the signage, and the packaging all exist. An afternoon of testing beats a rebrand.
