The short answer: open a PDF splitter, upload your file, pick the pages you want — a range like 5–12, or a few individual pages — and download them as a new PDF. The whole thing takes about a minute.

Splitting sounds technical, but it's one of the most ordinary document tasks there is. You've probably needed to do it without knowing the name for it: sending just one invoice out of a month's batch, pulling a single chapter from an ebook, or cutting the school permission slip out of a 40-page handbook.

## When you'd actually split a PDF

**Sending part of a document.** Your accountant asked for March's invoice, but your scanner saved January through June as one file. Split out the pages you need instead of sending everything.

**Extracting a chapter or section.** Ebooks, manuals, and reports are easier to read — and share — in pieces. Pull out the chapter you need and leave the 300-page rest behind.

**Job and school applications.** "Please upload your degree certificate" shouldn't mean uploading the entire 25-page transcript booklet. Extract the two relevant pages and send those.

**Cleaning up scans.** Scanned a stack of papers and got three documents in one file? Split them into their natural pieces so each file is one thing, with a name that says what it is.

## How to do it

### Step 1: Know your page numbers

Open the PDF and find the pages you want. Write down the range — say, pages 5 through 12. One gotcha: the page numbers printed on the document don't always match the PDF's page count. A report might start numbering at page 1 after four pages of cover material. Go by the PDF viewer's page counter, not the printed numbers.

### Step 2: Split it

Open PDFEdit's [split tool](/split-pdf), upload your file, and enter the pages you want. You can usually pick a continuous range (5–12), individual pages (3, 7, 9), or split the whole thing into separate one-page files. Download the result.

Everything happens in your browser — the file never gets uploaded anywhere, which is worth knowing when the pages contain bank statements or medical records.

### Step 3: Name the new file properly

"document-split.pdf" is a name you'll regret in a month. Call it what it is: "Q1-Invoices-Acme-Corp.pdf" or "Thesis-Chapter-3.pdf". Thirty seconds now, zero confusion later.

## Split vs. extract vs. delete — what's what

People use these words interchangeably, and most tools do all three, but they're slightly different operations:

- **Split** divides one PDF into several files. A 20-page PDF becomes, say, four 5-page PDFs.
- **Extract** pulls specific pages out into a new file and (usually) discards the rest. Extracting pages 5–12 is the same as splitting and keeping one piece.
- **Delete** removes pages from the original. If you want everything *except* pages 5–12, deleting those pages from a copy gets you there.

For most "I need just these pages" situations, extract is what you want. The [organize pages](/organize-pdf) tool covers the delete-and-rearrange side of things.

## Common mistakes

**Splitting the only copy.** Always work from a copy of the original, or at least keep the original file somewhere safe. Splitting is non-destructive when you extract to a new file, but if you delete pages from the original by accident, those pages are gone.

**Mixing up printed page numbers and PDF page numbers.** Worth repeating: trust the viewer's counter. Off-by-four errors are the classic way to send someone the wrong chapter.

**Extracting one page at a time.** If you need pages 5 through 12, enter the range once. Doing eight single-page extractions and then merging them back together works, but it's eight times the effort for the same result.

**Forgetting bookmarks and links.** Splitting keeps the page content intact, but document bookmarks and internal links that pointed across the split may break. For casual use this doesn't matter; for a technical manual it might. Worth a quick check if the document relies on its table of contents links.

## What survives the split

Splitting copies pages, so everything on those pages comes along: text, images, vector graphics, and formatting, exactly as they were. Quality doesn't drop a pixel — it's the same content in a smaller container.

A few things deserve a mention. Annotations and comments on the extracted pages usually carry over. Form field values typically survive too, though complex interactive forms can behave oddly once separated from the full document — test before you rely on it. And as noted above, bookmarks pointing to pages outside your selection will break, since those pages no longer exist in the new file.

Digital signatures are the one real casualty: a signed PDF that's split afterward will show the signature as invalid in the new files, because the underlying file content changed. If signatures matter, split first, then sign.

## Putting it back together

Splitting and [merging](/merge-pdf) are inverse operations, and you'll often use them as a pair: split a big file apart, work on the piece you need, merge it back. Or split three documents and merge selected pages from each into one custom file — a surprisingly common workflow for applications and reports. PDFEdit's merge tool handles that side in the same way: in the browser, no sign-up.

Once you're comfortable with both directions, most PDF assembly tasks become trivial. Need pages 3–5 from one file and the appendix from another? Split, split, merge, done. It's the kind of thing that feels like expertise the first time and like common sense every time after.
