The short answer: they're for different jobs. **Scanning apps** (Adobe Scan, Microsoft Lens, CamScanner) excel at *capturing* — they detect page edges, fix perspective, and clean up the image. **Browser converters** like PDFEdit's [Image to PDF](/tools/image-to-pdf) excel at *assembling* — turning images you already have into a clean PDF with no install, no account, and no watermark. Use the scanner app to capture well; use the browser tool to combine fast.

## What each one actually does

### Scanning apps: capture specialists

Point your camera at a page and the app:

- **Detects page edges** automatically and crops to the document
- **Corrects perspective** — a photo taken at an angle becomes a flat rectangle
- **Enhances the image** — boosts contrast, removes shadows, often a "document" filter
- **Captures multi-page** into one PDF in a guided flow
- **Sometimes OCRs** — premium tiers add searchable text

The catches: most are freemium. Free tiers commonly include watermarks on some exports, page or scan limits, upsells for OCR and cloud sync, mandatory accounts, and — worth knowing — many upload your scans to their cloud by default.

### Browser converters: assembly specialists

PDFEdit's Image to PDF tool:

- **Takes images you already have** — photos, screenshots, PNGs, JPGs, WebP, BMP, GIF
- **Combines them into one PDF** with visual drag-to-reorder
- **Embeds at full quality** — JPEGs pass through untouched, PNGs embed losslessly
- **Runs 100% in your browser** — no upload, no account, no watermark, no install

What it doesn't do: capture assistance. Your photos are your photos — no edge detection, no perspective fix, no enhancement. Garbage in, garbage out.

## When to use which

**Use a scanning app when:**
- You're photographing paper documents and want clean, flat, high-contrast page images
- Perspective and lighting are working against you (desk photos at angles)
- You want the guided multi-page capture flow
- You need in-app OCR and are willing to pay or accept the free tier's limits

**Use the browser converter when:**
- You already have the images (screenshots, photos, downloads)
- You want the fastest path from "folder of images" to "one PDF"
- You don't want another app, account, or subscription
- Privacy matters — the images never leave your device
- You're combining mixed formats (JPG + PNG + WebP in one batch)

**Use both when:**
- Capture with the scanner app for quality, export the page images, then assemble or re-order in the browser tool. Best of both worlds, and free.

## The quality question, honestly

A scanner app's edge detection and perspective correction genuinely produce better page images than freehand phone photos — straighter, cleaner, more readable. If you're archiving important documents, that capture quality is worth the app.

But once you have good images, the PDF assembly is a commodity: embedding a JPEG into a PDF is the same operation everywhere. There's no quality advantage to assembling in the scanner app versus the browser. So the decision is really about *capture*, not conversion.

## The privacy angle

This deserves emphasis because scanning apps handle disproportionately sensitive material — IDs, contracts, medical forms, receipts with account numbers. Many free scanner apps upload to their cloud for processing or sync by default. Check the app's settings; some let you work offline.

PDFEdit's converter processes everything locally in your browser — the images never leave your device. For sensitive captures, that's a meaningful difference. (And if a scan needs OCR afterward, PDFEdit's [OCR tool](/tools/ocr-pdf) is equally browser-local.)

## Common mistakes

**Photographing documents freehand when a scanner app would do better.** If the pages matter, use the capture assistance. Your shaky angled photo is not "good enough" for archival.

**Installing a scanner app just to combine screenshots.** Screenshots are already perfect digital images — they need assembly, not capture. Browser tool, thirty seconds, done.

**Paying for a subscription for occasional scans.** The free tier plus a browser assembler covers most people's actual scanning volume.

**Ignoring where free apps send your scans.** Check the privacy settings before photographing your passport.

## The hybrid workflow (best of both)

For important paper documents, combine them deliberately:

1. **Capture with a scanner app.** Adobe Scan and Microsoft Lens are both free and competent — edge detection, perspective correction, contrast enhancement. Capture all pages into the app.
2. **Export the page images** (or a PDF) from the app.
3. **Assemble/reorder in the browser** if needed — PDFEdit's Image to PDF for combining with other images, or the organize tool for reordering PDF pages.
4. **OCR afterward** with the browser OCR tool if you need the text — locally, no upload.

Total cost: zero. Total installs: one app you probably already have a use for. Quality: the best available without paid software.

## What the free tiers actually limit

Since "free" does heavy lifting in this comparison, specifics on the common scanner apps (as of writing — check current terms):

- **Adobe Scan:** free capture and PDF creation; premium subscription adds more OCR/export options and cloud storage.
- **Microsoft Lens:** free, no watermark, integrates with OneDrive/OneNote; OCR via Microsoft 365 features.
- **CamScanner:** free tier watermarks some outputs and pushes subscriptions fairly hard.

The pattern: capture is free everywhere; it's OCR, cloud features, and watermark-free exports where the upsells live. If you only need clean captures assembled into a PDF, you may never hit a paywall — especially combined with free browser assembly.
